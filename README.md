# Artefact 1: Understanding Intellectual Property

Summary of the Artefact

The first artefact is based on the Week 7 workshop material about Intellectual Property (IP). IP protects different types of creations and can include patents, trademarks, designs, copyright and trade secrets. The workshop explained that giving creators rights can encourage creativity and innovation, but society also benefits when inventions and creative works eventually become available to the public. Copyright in Australia is automatic and protects the way an original work is expressed rather than the information itself.

 Justification on Why I Chose the Artefact

I chose this artefact because Intellectual Property is relevant to ICT students and future technology work. Before the workshop, I mainly thought of copyright as protecting music and videos. I now understand that it can also protect databases, computer programs, photographs and other original works. This helped me see why ICT professionals need to understand IP when creating, sharing or using digital content.



# Artefact 2: The Monkey Selfie Copyright Dispute

## Summary of the Artefact

The second artefact is the monkey selfie copyright dispute. In 2011, photographer David Slater travelled to Sulawesi, Indonesia, where a crested macaque interacted with his camera and pressed the shutter. The photographs became widely known and raised the question of who owned their copyright. PETA later brought a case on behalf of the monkey Naruto. The US District Court dismissed the case because animals could not hold copyright under US law. The case is useful for discussing human authorship, non-human creativity and similar questions involving AI-generated works.

## Justification on Why I Chose the Artefact

I chose this case because it was one of the most unusual topics in the workshop and made me think about what it means to be an author. I found it interesting that simply taking a photograph can create a difficult legal question when a non-human animal is involved. It also connects to current questions about AI, because AI can generate creative-looking content without being a human creator.

## Article

**LicenseNet: Proactively Safeguarding Intellectual Property of AI Models Through Model License**

**Authors:** Peihao Li, Jie Huang and Shuaishuai Zhang

## Summary of the Artefact

This article discusses the importance of protecting the intellectual property of artificial intelligence (AI) models. The authors explain that AI models used on edge devices can be exposed to theft, misuse and tampering. They propose a framework called **LicenseNet**, which protects AI models by embedding license features into the models. The research shows that LicenseNet can improve security and resistance to fine-tuning attacks while reducing performance loss.

## Justification on Why I Chose the Artefact

I chose this article because it is closely related to the **Intellectual Property** topic covered in Workshop 7. It helped me understand that intellectual property protection is also important for modern technologies such as AI models. I found the article interesting because it explains a practical technical method for preventing unauthorised use and theft of AI models. It also helped me understand the connection between **AI, technology security and intellectual property protection**.

## My Personal Reflection

What I learnt this week was that Intellectual Property covers much more than I originally thought. The workshop introduced copyright, patents, trademarks and trade secrets and explained that each type of protection has a different purpose. I found copyright particularly relevant because digital works such as computer programs, databases, photographs and other creative works can be protected.

The monkey selfie case was interesting because it showed how copyright law can become complicated when technology or non-human actions are involved. It also made me think about AI-generated content. AI can produce images and other content, but the workshop explained that AI tools cannot currently own copyright. This made me think more carefully about who should receive rights when technology is involved in creating something.

I also learnt about fair dealing in Australia. It can allow copyrighted material to be used in specific circumstances, such as research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire and reporting news. I realised that finding something online does not mean that I can automatically copy or redistribute it.

Another topic I found useful was Digital Rights Management (DRM). DRM uses technologies such as encryption, passwords, paywalls, product keys and activation limits to control access to digital content. I understand why content owners use these protections, but I also understand the concerns about restrictions on legitimate use. Overall, the workshop showed me that IP involves finding a balance between protecting creators and providing benefits to society.



# Conclusion

This workshop improved my understanding of Intellectual Property and its importance in ICT. I learnt that copyright, patents, trademarks and trade secrets protect different types of intellectual property. The monkey selfie case and the discussion about AI showed me that new technology can create difficult questions for existing copyright principles. My main takeaway is that ICT professionals should respect intellectual property while also considering ethical issues and the wider benefits to society.
