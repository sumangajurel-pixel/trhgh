Intellectual Property (IP) is important because it provides protection for the work and ideas created by individuals and organisations. Intellectual property can include copyright, patents, trademarks, designs and trade secrets. The main purpose of IP protection is to encourage people to create and develop new ideas while also allowing society to benefit from those creations.

There needs to be a balance between protecting creators and allowing information and inventions to eventually become available to the public. The workshop explains that giving creators rights can stimulate creativity, but society can also benefit when inventions and creative works enter the public domain.

Copyright gives creators exclusive rights over original works. In Australia, copyright protection is automatic and does not require registration. It protects the way information is expressed rather than the information or idea itself.

Copyright can apply to many types of works, including literary works, emails, novels, song lyrics, databases and computer programs. It can also protect photographs, paintings, maps, music, films, sound recordings and broadcasts.

Copyright gives creators economic rights such as reproduction, publishing, public performance, communication and adaptation. Creators also have moral rights, including the right to be recognised as the author and to object to treatment of their work that damages their reputation. The workshop states that copyright generally lasts for **70 years after the death of the creator** in Australia.

## The Monkey Selfie Copyright Dispute

One interesting example discussed in the workshop is the **monkey selfie copyright dispute**. In 2011, photographer David Slater travelled to Sulawesi, Indonesia, where a crested macaque interacted with his camera and took photographs by pressing the shutter.

The photographs became widely known, and a legal question developed about who owned the copyright. Wikimedia Commons argued that the photographs should be public domain because the monkey took the photographs and copyright requires a human author.

PETA later filed a lawsuit on behalf of the monkey, Naruto, arguing that the monkey should own the copyright. The US District Court dismissed the case, stating that animals could not hold copyright under US law. The case became an important example when discussing authorship, non-human creativity and AI-generated works.

## Copyright and Artificial Intelligence

Artificial Intelligence creates new questions about intellectual property. The workshop explains that it is not always clear how copyright should apply to works created using AI. AI tools themselves do not currently have legal status as copyright owners.

This creates questions about authorship and ownership. For example, if an AI system creates an image, it can be difficult to determine who should own the copyright. Similar questions arise when AI tools are trained using protected works or when users use AI to modify existing creative content.

## Patents

A patent provides exclusive commercial rights for an invention, such as a device, substance, method or process. According to the workshop, an invention must be new, useful and inventive or innovative to qualify.

In Australia, a standard patent can last for up to 20 years, while an innovation patent is described in the workshop as lasting up to 8 years. Pharmaceutical patents can have a maximum duration of 25 years. Australian patents apply within Australia, so protection must be sought separately in other countries.

## Trademarks

A trademark is used to identify a unique product or service. It can include words, phrases, logos, pictures, sounds, shapes, smells, movements, packaging features or combinations of these elements.

Australian trademarks are registered for 10 years and can be renewed. Although registration is not required in every situation, the workshop recommends registration because it provides stronger protection. A business name, company name or domain name is not automatically a trademark.

## Trade Secrets

Trade secrets are confidential information that provides an organisation with a competitive advantage. They can include specialised information, know-how and business information.

Unlike patents and trademarks, trade secrets are not registered and do not have a fixed time limit. Organisations can protect this information through employee contracts, confidentiality agreements and non-disclosure agreements (NDAs).

## Fair Use and Fair Dealing

Copyright does not always prevent people from using copyrighted material. Certain exceptions allow material to be used without permission.

The workshop compares **fair use in the United States** with **fair dealing in Australia**. US fair use can include purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship and research. Factors include the purpose of the use, the nature of the copyrighted work, the amount used and the effect on the potential market.

Australian fair dealing is more restricted and includes areas such as research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, news reporting and legal or professional advice.

## Digital Rights Management

Digital Rights Management (DRM) refers to technologies used by copyright owners to control access to digital content. DRM can use encryption, content marking and other technological protections.

Examples of technological protection mechanisms include passwords, paywalls, product keys, activation limits, time limits, limits on simultaneous users and encryption.

DRM can help protect intellectual property, but it can also create problems. The workshop identifies concerns such as restrictions on fair use, reduced competition and limitations on anonymous access.

## Software Protection

Software can be protected through copyright, patents and licences. In Australia, source code is treated as a literary work and can receive copyright protection.

Copyright violations can include copying software to give or sell to another person, preloading software onto a computer being sold and distributing software over the Internet.

Software licensing is another important form of protection. Proprietary licences generally provide users with rights to use software under specific conditions. Free and open-source software licences can provide additional rights to use, copy and modify software. Public domain software has no copyright restrictions and provides users with full rights.

## Ethical Reflection

Intellectual property protection provides value to creators because it can encourage innovation and creativity. However, excessive protection can also restrict society's access to information and technology.

From an ethical perspective, there should be a balance between the rights of creators and the interests of society. For example, software developers need protection so they have an incentive to create new software, but users may also benefit from open-source software that allows people to study, modify and share code.

The workshop also demonstrates that ethics and law are not always the same. Something may be legally permitted but still raise ethical questions. This is particularly important with AI, digital content, software copying and DRM.

## Conclusion

Intellectual Property is an important part of ICT because it protects creative works, inventions, brands, confidential information and software. Copyright, patents, trademarks and trade secrets provide different forms of protection depending on the type of intellectual property.

The workshop also shows that technology creates new challenges for IP law. Issues such as AI-generated content, digital rights management, software licensing and online distribution require careful consideration. Overall, IP protection should provide appropriate rewards for creators while also considering the benefits and rights of society.
